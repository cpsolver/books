caption-begin
Fractions are for communication
----


image-description-begin
mouth speaking fraction "two thirds" to ear

caption-begin
When someone requests filling their glass "three fourths full," that's clear.  In contrast, if that person said to fill it "seventy five percent full," that would be confusing, or snobbish, or both.
----

caption-begin
This example reveals that fractions are very useful for communication.
----

caption-begin
Fractions are especially useful in recipies, such as telling you to combine a half teaspoon of salt with one and a half cups of flour.
----

caption-begin
However, calculating with fractions should never be needed.
----

caption-begin
This brings us to the third big math secret.  A fraction is a division task that has not yet been done.
----

caption-begin
This means you should do the division for each fraction before you combine numeric fractions using addition or subtraction.
----

caption-begin
Unfortunately schools waste countless hours teaching students to do the opposite.  They teach kids how to add and subtract fractions.  That's not a useful skill in real life.
----

caption-begin
You might disagree if you are a carpenter who uses a tape measure, or if you sew fabrics to make clothes.  In these cases, please keep reading because the next secret will save you lots of time.
----

caption-begin
Tricks for adding and subracting fractions arose many centuries ago.  Those tricks, which include looking for the least common denominator, were needed because ancient people didn't know how to divide three by four.
----

caption-begin
Yet now we we use decimal numbers, not Roman numerals or counting marks.  So it's easy to divide three by four.  The result is zero point seven five, or seventy five percent.
----

caption-begin
So if you need to use a fraction in a calculation, start by dividing the two numbers in the fraction.  Remember a number expressed as a fraction is a division task that has not yet been done.
----

caption-begin
In contrast, if the fraction is being used for communication, there's no need to do division.
----

keyframe_inches_begin.svg

caption-begin
Biggest Math Secret Number Four
----

caption-begin
Decimal inches are superior to fractional inches
----

image-description-begin
decimal ruler, ten inches
----

caption-begin
Possibly the biggest loss of joy in math education happens when young students learn to use a ruler marked in quarters, eighths, and possibly sixteenths of an inch.
----

caption-begin
Splitting an inch into quarters and eighths has been obsolete for at least a few centuries.  Yet students in the United States are required to buy rulers with these obsolete markings.
----

caption-begin
And some adult professions still require employees to use fractional inches.  Carpentry and sewing are two such professions.
----

caption-begin
Either as a student or an employee, if you still use rulers or tape measures marked with fractions, it's time to buy a ruler and tape measure with marks for decimal inches.
----

caption-begin
You will be amazed at how much easier it is to add and subtract decimal inches.
----

caption-begin
Some educators think we should avoid fractional inches by switching to the metric system.  Nope!  That misunderstanding is explained in the next math secret.
----

caption-begin
Instead schools should be asking students to buy a ruler that shows decimal inches, where each interval between marks is one tenth of an inch.
----

caption-begin
Students need to learn the pattern of decimal intervals.  It's easy to find the larger tic mark at half an inch.  The smaller tic marks in the first half an inch are numbered one through four.  The remaining four smaller tic marks are numbered six through nine.
----

caption-begin
This same pattern, but in a smaller size, appears on a ruler marked in centimeters.  So learning decimal inches makes it much easier to learn the metric system.
----

caption-begin
Here's another important change.  School rulers should go up to ten inches, not twelve inches.
----

caption-begin
When the topic of percentages arise, the ten-inch ruler will have provided a useful way to judge percentages from zero to one hundred.  Students will immediately realize each tenth of an inch is simply one percent of the ruler's total marked length.
----

caption-begin
As a further advantage, a ten-inch decimal ruler provides a handy way to teach young children how to add and subtract integers up to one hundred.  Addition goes to the right.  Subtraction goes to the left.  The bigger marks keep track of the tens digit, the smaller tic marks keep track of the units digit.  Although this exercise can be done on a foot-long ruler, the extra two inches distract from the simplicity of the decimal system.
----

caption-begin
Workers in the construction industry already can buy a "decimal tape measure," which is sometimes called an "engineering" tape measure.
----

caption-begin
Alas, for people who do sewing, cloth tape measures marked in decimal inches are currently very hard to find.  That will change soon.
----

caption-begin
Of course lots of instructional videos and written materials still require an understanding of fractions of an inch.  That's not a problem.  Switching between decimal inches and fractional inches is easy.  Just use a tape measure that shows both fractional and decimal inches.  When you need to convert, just look across to the opposite side, the one with the different markings.  Ideally you should measure and calculate using decimal inches.  Then when you receive a fractional number, or need to supply a fractional number to someone else, just look across the tape measure.
----

caption-begin
Here's one more clarification about using rulers with decimal inches.  It will prepare them for getting a ruler printed on paper that goes from negative five inches at the left to positive five inches at the right, with zero in the middle.  Exercises that count the decimal tic marks can temporarily take them to the left of the zero mark, before returning to positive numbers.  That exposure to temporary negative numbers will help prepare them for later learning how to add and subtract negative numbers.
----

caption-begin
Let's stop wasting lots of school time, and stop taking the joy out of math.  Stop insisting that inches must be measured using fractions.  That skill became obsolete centuries ago when decimal numbers arrived.
----

caption-begin
Biggest Math Secret Number Five
----

caption-begin
Units, pick one for calculations, use any for measuring and communication
----

image-description-begin
two teaspoons of salt and four cups of flour
----

caption-begin
Europeans use the metric system, which measures time in seconds.  Yet two European friends don't say "Bye, see you in six hundred and four point eight kiloseconds" instead of saying "Bye, see you in a week."  Everyone, worldwide, speaks using the same familiar time units we use in the United States, namely minutes, hours, days, weeks, months, and years.
----

caption-begin
Some people think we should adopt metric units in the kitchen for food prep.  Yet that misses the point.  Recipes can continue to specify quantities in tablespoons, teaspoons, and cups.  And we can continue to conveniently specify other food quantities in pints, quarts, and liters.
----

caption-begin
And we can continue to use yards for football fields, and feet for cutting lumber, and inches for clothing sizes.
----

caption-begin
Only when we need to do calculations do we need to choose just one unit for each measurement category.
----

caption-begin
This concept is similar to the distinction between fractions and decimal numbers.  Fractions are fine for communication, but decimals are needed for calculations.  In a similar way, any unit can be used for communication, but choosing just one unit per category is necessary for doing calculations.
----

caption-begin
Many scientists promote the idea of switching to the metric system.  Yet if a reporter asks a scientist how long it will take a spacecraft to reach the moon, the scientific answer expressed in seconds isn't useful.  Instead the time in seconds needs to be converted into days to be meaningful for non-scientists.  It's the computers guiding the spacecraft that need to exclusively use seconds for all calculations.
----

caption-begin
Some people promote the idea of using the metric system for kitchen measurements of volume.  Yet even Europeans who use the metric system often measure food quantities using a weight scale, not by using containers measured in cubic millimeters, cubic centimeters, or decimal liters.
----

caption-begin
If food ingredients are measured using an electronic weight scale, an expensive scale is needed.  That's because it must be accurate for both large quantities, such as flour for baking, and small quantities, such as for salt and spices.  Getting these proportions wrong is devastating for baking.
----

caption-begin
Cheap plastic measuring scoops and cups easily handle this dramatic range of volume.
----

caption-begin
Some people might misunderstand what's needed to make this change.  There's no need to modify any cooking recipies.  Instead just take a picture or screenshot of any recipie and ask artificial intelligence to convert it, including all the measurements in all the steps, into any units you want.
----

caption-begin
When you don't have a computerized way to convert units, here's a tip.  You can multiply a number times the equivalent of the number one.  For example, sixty minutes per hour is essentially the number one.  So you can mulitiply sixty times a number in miles per minute to convert into miles per hour.
----

caption-begin
Let's teach students to communicate using whatever measuring unit fits the situation.  And let's teach students that calculations require converting each supplied number of the same type into the same unit.  Then, after calculating, that unit can be be converted back into any meaningful unit for communication.
----

keyframe_triangles_begin.svg

caption-begin
Biggest Math Secret Number Six
----

caption-begin
Triangles deserve deep understanding
----

image-description-begin
three-dimensional, two parallel lines and two crossing lines forming two triangles
----

caption-begin
Even before children learn how to add and subtract they can draw three lines on a sheet of paper.  And they can be taught to draw these lines along a straightedge, which can be a piece of wood or plastic that has no numbers or markings.  And they can recognize the shape of three intersecting lines as a triangle.
----

caption-begin
At that point they are ready to learn to draw a map.  Indoors the map can be of the classroom or an assembly hall.  Outdoors the map can be of fenceposts and building corners, and possibly trees.
----

caption-begin
To draw this map there must be two tables that are not too close to each other.  On a piece of paper the child should draw two Xs where they imagine the tables will be located on their completed map.  Then, using a straightedge, they need to draw a line between those two Xs.
----

caption-begin
At each table the child rotates the paper so that first line points in the direction of the other table.  Then the child draws lines that point to distant places such as room corners and fenceposts.  These lines must pass through the X that represents the table they are currently at.
----

caption-begin
That same process is repeated at the other table.
----

caption-begin
The place where two lines to the same location cross is the location of that wall's corner, or fencepost, or tree.  The walls and fences can be shown by drawing a straight line between the two points that connect with a wall or fence.
----

caption-begin
After these children have later learned how to divide two numbers, they can use their decimal rulers to measure distances on their maps.  And they can use division and multiplication to measure actual distances without being able to walk along those measured distances.
----

caption-begin
This experience can become more enjoyable by letting them practice their algebraic mystery-solving skills to figure out how to measure whatever distances they choose.
----

caption-begin
And they will quickly learn they can measure the height of a tree or building by using a triangle oriented vertically.
----

caption-begin
Here the math secret is that a deep understanding of triangles at an early age will make it much easier to understand troublesome math topics including division, trigonometry, graphing, geometry, physics, and even calculus.
----

caption-begin
Consider that the trigonometric functions named sine, cosine, tangent, arcsine, arccosine, and arctangent are just ratios among the sides of a right triangle.
----

caption-begin
Consider that graphing linear functions is equivalent to using the edges of the paper to play the role of the X and Y axes.  This means a specific point on the paper has an X value equal to the the distance to the paper's left edge, and has a Y value equal to the distance to the paper's bottom edge.
----

caption-begin
Consider that triangles are foundational concepts in geometry.  This preparation will allow high-school geometry teachers to more quickly teach college-entrance geometry topics, such as the two-column layout of math proofs, the cleverest steps in Euclid's proofs, and non-Euclidean geometry.
----

caption-begin
Consider that one of the concepts in physics is called the inclined plane.  That's simply the ratio between horizontal distances and vertical distances.
----

caption-begin
Consider that the calculus concept called a derivative, which is expressed as dy divided by dx, can be thought of as the height of a right triangle divided by the width of that triangle.
----

caption-begin
Let's start teaching foundational concepts about triangles much earlier.  Let's not delay the subject until high school when triangles are so important to advanced courses in geometry, trigonometry, physics, and calculus.
----

caption-begin
Biggest Math Secret Number Seven

caption-begin
Math history enhances understanding math
----

image-description-begin
Ancient Roman scratching head, holding scroll showing fraction VI/XIV
----

caption-begin
Which math event led to the decline of highway robbery and the begining of the Italian Renessance?
----

caption-begin
Why do hotels not have a thirteenth floor?
----

caption-begin
Why were logarithms especially useful for a couple of centuries?
----

caption-begin
Is learning algebra's "order of operations" really an important math skill?
----

caption-begin
Young students deserve to learn the math history that hides behind these and other math-related questions.
----

caption-begin
Consider the first question.  Highway robbery was a normal part of travel back when Romans built the first highways.  There were no banks in Europe to keep track of money flowing between European cities.  So merchants and wealthy people had to carry coins, jewels, and other valuable objects when they traveled between cities.
----

caption-begin
At that time math in Italy was done using Roman numerals.  The transition to the digits zero through nine was popularized around the year one thousand when a Bishop named Gerbert of Aurillac became Pope Sylvester the Second.  He had learned to use Arabic numerals and recognized their superiority.  In contrast, prior Popes had declared it was a sin to use Arabic numerals.
----

caption-begin
That math event made it affordable for wealthy Italian bankers to hire enough accountants to track wealth transfers between cities.  This development included making it easy to audit the accountants so they didn't steal coins as they counted them.  This is why we can drive down a deserted highway and not worry about highway robbery.  Unlike coins and jewels, highway robbers can't use the bank documents we carry.
----

caption-begin
As a further benefit, travel between cities increased dramatically, which increased business activities and increased the flow of ideas, both of which helped lead to the Italian Renesainse.
----

caption-begin
This historic example reveals to young children why math is so important to learn.  An understanding of math leads to advantageous innovations.
----

caption-begin
As a bonus part of this history lesson, students can experience how difficult it is to divide two numbers using Roman numerals.  That will help students understand why early Europeans learned to add and subtract fractions instead of just doing the division calculations implied by the fractions.  And they will appreciate that multiplying and dividing isn't as difficult as it was for people in the ancient past.
----

caption-begin
Interestingly, in the future, when students learn the awkwardness of adding and subtracting fractions, even using the digits zero through nine, they will wonder why those of us living today are still measuring inches using fractions, even though fractional inches should have become obsolete centuries ago.
----

caption-begin
Perhaps one reason math history isn't explicity taught is it might reveal some of our current math education traditions to be embarrassingly obsolete.
----

caption-begin
If you're wondering why logarithms were especially useful for a couple of centuries, it's because it provided a shortcut for doing division and multiplication.  For awhile this trick was done using printed tables of numbers.  Then it was done using a slide rule.  Now that everyone has a computer in their pocket, this logarithmic trick has become obsolete.
----

caption-begin
If you're wondering why hotels don't have thirteenth floors, consider that getting stuck dividing the number thirteen by any other number is frustrating, and therefore would have been unlucky back before the digits zero through nine arrived.  That frustration still shows up in the English language because numbers ten, eleven, and twelve have special names, but starting at thirteen we switch to the "teen" naming convention.  The centuries-old reputation of thirteen as unlucky would make the thirteenth floor of a hotel a tempting target for mischevious young troublemakers.
----

caption-begin
To appreciate why algebra's "order of operations" notation was invented, consider how ancient mathemeticians wrote math equations.  One method was to write equations in sawdust or sand sprinkled on a table.  When printing technology was adopted, it was useful to keep equations small to reduce paper consumption.  When quill pens were used for writing, fewer dips of the pen into an inkwell was an advantage.  Those limiations have been obsolete for centuries.  So when you "do math" as an adult, use as many parentheses as you want to keep track of the order in which operations need to be done.
----

caption-begin
Math history, if taught well, is not only facinating and insightful, it's inspiring.  So let's use math-history stories to inspire joy in students.  They can appreciate their struggles are tiny compared to what scholars experienced centuries ago.
----

caption-begin
Biggest Math Secret Number Eight
----

caption-begin
Calculus and advanced algebra are valuable for scientists, mathematicians, and the testing industry
----

caption-begin
Copernicus with planet device, with planets and ellipses swirling in background
----

caption-begin
Calculus is breathtakingly beautiful.  Using it led to being able to calculate the exact position of a planet at any moment in time.  Integrals allow us to exactly calculate the area under a polynomial curve.  Derivatives allow us to exactly calculate the speed of a falling object at every moment during its fall.
----

caption-begin
Yet climbing the "advanced-placement" mountain to glimpse the beauty of calculus consumes too much time, effort, and money considering that very very few professions require using calculus for any calculations.
----

caption-begin
In a similar way, advanced algebra is filled with beautiful concepts that involve vectors, matrices, advanced trigonometry, and imaginary numbers.  Yet those topics also aren't used in hardly any professions.
----

caption-begin
So why have schools built a well-traveled road to the top of a mountain for a quick view of the mathematical beauty in calculus and advanced algebra?  There are two reasons.
----

caption-begin
The more noble reason is the hope of training future scientists who will pioneer advances in software, electronics, biochemistry, satellites, and space travel.  And they want to train future engineers who will pioneer new products, better bridges, and faster computers.  These frontiers have become the battlegrounds for nations competing against each other for economic advantages.  This is why lots of tax money is spent on education for training scientists and engineers.
----

caption-begin
The uglier reason for focusing on seldom-used math concepts is that lots of businesses earn lots of money testing students on the advanced math topics of calculus, advanced algebra, and advanced trigonometry.  Additional businesses profit from preparing students to be successful when they take these college-entrance exams.  And universities can gain prestige and charge higher tuition if more of their graduates enter high-paying professions that involve higher levels of math.

caption-begin
Fortunately, as previously explained, the most useful parts of calculus and advanced algebra can be moved into earlier grade levels.  This approach will free up time for high-school students to learn real-life math topics that are currently untaught, yet useful.  Those math topics should include surveying, pairwise counting, and the linear algebra that is a foundational part of artificial intelligence.

caption-begin
Terminology and precise definitions seldom convey insightful concepts
----

image-description-begin
image: student, spelling bee setting, saying "an improper fraction is ..."

caption-begin
When scientists work on solving a math challenge, rarely do they think in words such as "here's a variable" and "here's the unknown quantity."  When engineers design a bridge that uses triangles for stability, they don't name the trianges as "isocoles" or "right triangle."  And only a few people remember the name "Pythagorean theorem" when they remember that, for a right triangle, the sum of the squares of the two shorter sides equals the square of the longer side.
----

caption-begin
So why do math textbooks and standardized tests put so much emphasis on terminology?
----

caption-begin
Up until a few decades ago, before videos and audio recordings became inexpensive to produce, watch, and listen to, knowledge was written in books.  An easy way to find out if a student had read a chapter in a textbook was to ask questions about the definitions of terms defined in the chapter.
----

caption-begin
For this reason, textbook authors, who were experts in their academic fields, were told to include terminology that they, the experts, did not themselves use in their own academic field.
----

caption-begin
Alas, public education, including math education, now wastes excessive amounts of textbook space and classroom time teaching terminology with carefully worded definitions.  That terminology will not be useful to most people when they are in the worlds of business, employment, travel, entertainment, preparing meals, and government.
----

caption-begin
Instead it's the concepts behind the words that are important to learn.
----

caption-begin
Earlier you learned that division is hidden in words such as proportion, scale, ratio, rate, slope, and reciprocal.  The words themselves are not needed for solving real challenges.  Yet the underlying concepts they convey are extremely valuable.
----

caption-begin
Of course there are lots of professions where terminology is very important when communicating.  Fortunately Wikipedia and artificial-intelligence services can quickly supply the correct term, and its carefully worded definition, when needed.
----

caption-begin
Math education should reduce the number of math terms being taught in school.  Let's update video and audio learning resources so they don't make the same mistake as textbooks, namely putting an excessive focus on terminology.  Instead, let's help them focus on clearly understanding the underlying concepts.
----

Multiple choice tests are obsolete.  This is the next math secret.

Rather than wasting time looking at the many limitations of multiple-choice tests, let's consider the question "What will replace multiple-choice tests?"

Those tests will be replaced by computer software that uses elements of artificial intelligence to provide each and every math student with a private math tutor.  That software will be free, tiny, and capable of both teaching and testing at the same time.

caption-begin
Biggest Math Secret Number Eleven
----

caption-begin
Math notation needs updating to match digital and audio technology
----

image-description-begin
person speaking to ear with cupped hand, "a squared minus x divided by square root of b"
----

caption-begin
You've probably seen the math equation E equals M C squared.  Notice the spoken version of this equation uses the word "squared."  In contrast, the written version writes the digit two as a superscript written in a smaller font.  This difference between spoken and written math notation needs to be clearly explained to students.
----

caption-begin
Yet also, students need permission to stop writing obsolete notations.  Especially when that notation cannot be typed using a computer keyboard.  And especially now that speaking to artificial intelligence agents does not have a useful way to indicate superscript formatting, not to mention font size changes.
----

caption-begin
The secret is that the math notation taught in school needs to be updated to match how humans now communicate.  It needs to match how people speak to each other.  And it needs to match how we communicate with artificial intelligence technology, which arrived only a few years ago.
----

caption-begin
Why is this change needed?  Human communication has suddenly changed in just a few generations.  Until the early nineteen hundreds we had no convenient and affordable way to record human speech.  The best alternative was written words.
----

caption-begin
Affordable voice recording technology didn't arrive until less than a century ago.  Now, nearly every student carries a phone that easily records their voice, and any other nearby voice.  And those voice recordings can be shared instantly worldwide.
----

caption-begin
In prior centuries, writing on paper and printing on paper was the only affordable and convenient way to communicate math equations.  As a result, the notation for math equations developed in ways that are awkward to speak, yet convenient to write.  And the number of pen strokes was significant because ink pens had to be frequently dipped into an inkwell to get more ink.
----

caption-begin
A few early mechanical and electric typewriters could type a superscript on paper.  But that option disappeared when computer keyboards arrived.  Modern keyboards have no superscript key or font-size key.
----

caption-begin
We need to update some math notation conventions.  Already, computer programming languages have adopted the convention of using two asterisks to indicate raising a numeric value to a power.  This means C squared is written as C asterisk asterisk two.
----

caption-begin
Spoken communication does not have a convenient way to specify lowercase and uppercase letters.  Yet lots of math notation relies on a significant difference between lowercase and uppercase letters.
----

caption-begin
For example, in the equation E equals M C squared, the E being capitalized and the M and C being lowercase are significant.  Saying "uppercase E equals lowercase M times lowercase C squared" is awkward and distracting.
----

caption-begin
It's easier to say "energy equals mass times speed of light squared."  That takes longer to write.  That's why math notation is full of abbreviations.  And why math explanations often begin with "let E represent energy, let M equal mass, and let C equal the speed of light."
----

caption-begin
We need to begin to update math notation so it matches curent technology.
----

caption-begin
As a start, math teachers need to admit that math notation is becoming obsolete for non-math professions.  Without this secret being revealed, students assume they are to blame for struggling to learn math notation.
----

caption-begin
Scientists, mathematicians, and some engineers do need to continue to use traditional math notation.  That doesn't need to change.  But students going into other professions should be allowed to learn math concepts without insisting on only learning a notation system that is only used by scientists, mathematicians, and a few other professionals.
----

caption-begin
And we need to allow students to write the spoken version of math.  For example, "raised to the power of two" should be allowed as an alternative to using the digit two as a superscript.
----

caption-begin
Here's a bigger gap betwen written and spoken notation.  Math requires clearly indicating which group of things are related to which other group of things.  For example, this math expression uses a long horizontal line to show which values are divided by which other values.  The spoken version needs to indicate the beginning of a group of values and the corresponding end of a group of values.  Computer programming languages use parentheses for this grouping.  And the groups can be nested using nested parentheses.
----

caption-begin
Eventually new words will be adopted to clarify grouping.  As an example, the Dashrep programming lanugage uses the words "ambee" and "amenn" to indicate the beginning and ending of a nested group.  Some software developers use the words "open paren" and "close paren" to indicate grouping, but that's awkward.  And it's awkward when people speak to Siri or Alexa.
----

caption-begin
Insisting that all students must reduce the use of parentheses when they solve an algebra mystery is another huge barrier to learning the underlying concepts of algebra.  Yet that's the primary purpose of the order of operations.  These tricks to avoid writing as many parentheses was useful back when algebraic equations were written by hand using ink on paper, chalk on chalkboards, or sand or dust sprinkled on a table.  But those resource limitations are long gone.
----

caption-begin
It makes sense that college-entrance exams expect students to know the superscript notation for raising a number to a power.  That's because those tests are designed to idenfity which students have studied lots and lots of math.  But math can be learned without studying lots and lots of math.  The time spent learning nearly obsolete notation conventions would be better spent solving real-life math problems.
----

caption-begin
To make math concepts more accessible, we should allow students to choose from among a number of different notation options.  Some students may prefer the written visual options used by mathematicians.  Other students may prefer auditory spoken options, which are still being developed.  Yet other students may prefer software conventions or spreadsheet conventions.
----

caption-begin
Let students choose.  Don't insist they can only use the notation conventions used by mathematicians and scientists.
----

caption-begin
The next time you look at a math textbook and see this division sign, the one with a horizontal line and a dot above it and a dot below the line, consider that computer keyboards do not have that key.  So why do we wait until high school to give them permission to stop writing that obsolete symbol?  And why do we insist students have to remember that the letters M and C next to each other mean those numbers are multiplied?  And why can't students be allowed to write parentheses around the C squared part to indicate that calculation is done before the multiplication?
----

caption-begin
Statistics and probability are misunderstood counting shortcuts
----

image-description-begin
normal curve with colorful segments
----

caption-begin
Suppose you don't know how to swim.  Would you choose to wade across a river that has an average depth of sixteen inches?  Would your choice change if the median depth is also sixteen inches?  Would also knowing the standard deviation help you decide?
----

caption-begin
If you have a financial investment that increases by fifteen percent, and then decreases by fifteen percent, have you gained money, lost money, or are you back to your original dollar amount?
----

caption-begin
How do you judge the difference between a thirty percent chance of rain and a fourty percent chance of showers?
----

caption-begin
Statistics courses spend lots of time teaching how to characterize lots of data by reducing it to just a few numbers.  Sometimes that's very useful.  Often it's misleading.
----

caption-begin
Consider the question about wading across a river that measures sixteen inches on average.  What you really need is a map.  Ideally it would show you the river depth at various locations.  Even better, it would be a topographic map of water depth.  Then you will know where it's safe to wade cross.  Notice the numbers for average, median, and standard deviation aren't useful for this purpose.
----

caption-begin
Averages, medians, and standard deviations serve to characterize what's called a normal distribution.  It's also called a bell curve.
----

caption-begin
Yet real life seldom involves normal distributions.  In fact, when you are trying to solve a real-life problem, it's likely a normal distribution does not apply.  If it did, the problem would be much easier to solve.  If it's easy to solve, you aren't likely to get hired solving such simple problems.
----

caption-begin
Mathematical simplicity is worth studying.  Yet this is just a beginning.
----

caption-begin
Consider the weather-related question about percent chance of rain and percent chance of showers.  A shower has lots of rain, but a rain shower covers only a relatively small area.  That shower can pass by you a few miles away.
----

caption-begin
Again what's needed is a map.  In this case a weather map.  Ideally an up-to-date radar map that shows where rain is falling.  The isolated areas of shower "activity" and their direction become easy to see, and much easier to consider for whether you need to wear a rain jacket.
----

caption-begin
Even after studying statistics, most people have to think carefully about what happens when there's an increase of fifteen percent followed by a decrease of fifteen percent.  This deeper kind of thinking is needed.  Yet lots of people who struggle with math assume the percent numbers can be added and subtracted, so they cancel out.  It takes deeper thinking to understand that an increase of fifteeen percent followed by a decrease of fifteen percent ends with a smaller number.
----

caption-begin
Numbers are great.  They give us information.  Yet numbers, especially statistics, also make it easy to believe we know more than what we actually know.
----

caption-begin
Math does not get in the way of reaching deep understanding.  Yet having numbers is just a beginning.  We constantly need to recognize what those numbers really mean.
----

caption-begin
Solving real-life problems requires gaining insights.  And insights don't come from just applying some statistics calculations.  We still need to dig deep to reach a clear understanding.
----

caption-begin
Biggest Math Secret Number Thirteen
----

caption-begin
Geometry is beautiful except when it's used for torture
----

image-description-begin
spirograph and beautiful equation plot
----

caption-begin
What is the area of a triangle?  The torturous way to teach the answer to this question is to teach students to memorize this formula:  "the area of a triangle is one-half its height times its base."  Most of us fail to remember this formula because geometry is about images and this formula is a very specific set of words.
----

caption-begin
So here's a simple way to draw a sketch that arrives at this formula, but without using words.  Sketch any triangle so the longest side of the triangle is at the bottom, meaning the bottom line is horizontal.  Then draw a horizontal line that goes through the top point of the rectangle.  Then draw two vertical lines that go through the points at each end of the bottom line.  You now have a rectangle that encloses the triangle.  To see how the area of the triangle compares to the area of the rectangle, draw a third vertical line that goes through the top triangle point.
----

caption-begin
Now it's easy to see the left side of the triangle is half the area of the left half of the big rectangle, and the right side of the triangle is half the area of the right side of the rectangle.  This relationship makes it obvious that you can calculate the triangle's area by first calculating the rectangle's area, and then dividing by two.
----

caption-begin
Since geometry is about lines, why does the teaching of geometry involve so many words?  And so much terminology?  And so many precise definitions?  And formal "proofs" that have words in two columns?
----

caption-begin
Video animations much more easily convey the principles of geometry.  Such animations are limited because video technology was extremely expensive to create until a few decades ago.  Now that it has become easy to create animated videos, it's time to update the teaching of geometry.
----

caption-begin
The big secret here is that geometry is much easier to understand using drawings and animations, and much harder to understand using words.
----

caption-begin
Yet geometry is taught using words because standarized tests must ask questions using words.  And because producing images and videos, until recently, required expensive resources and specialized skills.
----

caption-begin
Teaching basic geometry to young students will bring more joy to their education.
----

caption-begin
For example, imagine teaching young children how to draw a five-pointed star where the five points are on a circle.  Then imagine teaching them how to draw stars with more points by measuring smaller chords within the circle.  Then imagine teaching them to choose other chord lengths that produce lines that do not return to the starting point.  Those students have just learned the concept that some numbers can produce an infinite number of lines, which gives them a graphical way to understand the concept of prime numbers.  Prime numbers have become important for creating secret codes and implementing cryptocurrency.
----

caption-begin
Yet typically schools delay teaching geometry until high school.  Why?
----

caption-begin
Young students would experience lots of joy sketching triangles, triangles within circles, and amazingly beautiful shapes.  That joy would inspire many young students to learn more about angles and lines.  That learning will make it easier to understand the terminology and notation taught in high school geometry classes.
----

caption-begin
Of course it's important to teach older students word-oriented aspects of geometry.  That can be taught in high school, when students are closer to taking college-entrance exams that require understanding that terminology, their associated precise definitions, and specialized notation that fit the limitations of standardized tests.
----

caption-begin
As an example, college-entrance tests want to know if a student understands the Pythagorean theorem.  Young students will already have learned there are multiple ways to prove the long length of a right triangle is easy to calculate because the square of that length equals the sum of the squares of the triangle's two other side lengths.  This mathematical relationship was discovered thousands of years ago, long before Pythagoreas used the new technology of writing to publish the first description of this formula.
----

caption-begin
As explained in an earlier secret, developing a clear understanding of triangles is a path to better understanding math.  Triangles are almost half the basis of geometry because two-dimensional shapes formed with straight lines can be understood as a collection of related smaller triangles.  Another big part of geometry is circles.
----

caption-begin
The basic principles of geometry should be taught early.  The notation and terminology can be taught later.

Let's take advantage of the beauty and joy of geometry.  Let's teach its fundamentals to young students in ways that convey the joy and beauty of geometry.  Later, in high school, students can learn the terminoloy, notation, and proofs that make it harder to appreciate its joy and beauty.
----

caption-begin
This shift in teaching is easy to achive now that computer software makes it easy to create memorable drawings and animations that convey foundational and joyful insights in the field of geometry.
----
