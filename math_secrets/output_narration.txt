13 Biggest Secrets of Math

If you think math is difficult to learn,

it's probably because schools failed to

teach you these thirteen math secrets.

Fortunately it's not too late

to learn these secrets.

It doesn't matter if you are young or old.

It doesn't matter how much math

you did or didn't learn in school.

And it doesn't matter whether you think

you do or don't know how to

"do math" when numbers are involved.

Shortly you will understand why

current math education methods

fail to bring joy and simplicity

to math education.

Better yet, just by watching this video

you will quickly gain valuable new math insights

you can apply the next time you need to "do math."

Plus, these math insights

provide real-life advantages

over artificial intelligence,

even though AI easily aces

college-level math exams.

Let's start this journey now.

Biggest Math Secret Number One

Here's the first biggest math secret:

Algebra is about solving mysteries.

The great mathematician Albert Einstein,

when he was a child, struggled when

he was first learning algebra.

Fortunately a wise uncle advised Albert

to think of algebra as a mystery

where the purpose is to become a detective

and discover the hidden identity of a Mister X.

Imagine the fun children could have

if elementary-school teachers presented

mysteries and puzzles with clues,

semi-secret knowledge, and hidden insights.

The basics of algebra can be,

and should be, taught

starting at an early age.

Soon after learning addition and subtraction,

kids can learn about the equal sign,

and it's usefulness for

keeping track of math clues.

And kids are ready to learn that

adding and subtracting the same number

to or from both sides of that equal sign

is a useful way to calculate an unknown quantity.

A couple of years later they can learn

the trick of multiplying and dividing

both sides of an equation by the same number.

These simple math tricks

are the foundations of algebra.

During this early learning

it's important to allow children to use

either uppercase or lowercase letters

to represent unknown values.

To avoid confusing the letter X

with the child-level use of X

for multiplication, kids should be

introduced to the asterisk symbol.

That's what calculators, spreadsheets,

and computer programming languages

use to indicate multiplication.

Young children are not yet ready to learn

the concept of a "constant."

That's an unknown number that cannot

be calculated from the available clues.

That concept can wait for high school.

And they should not yet be introduced to

algebra's "order of operations."

Instead, rectangles can show groupings.

Kids do need to learn about negative numbers.

Shortly you'll learn how those can be introduced.

This early teaching of mystery-solving algebra

during grade school will teach students

how to solve math mysteries based on

clues and facts that are sometimes confusing.

This skill is useful in lots of professions.

In contrast, lots of notation and terminology

now taught in high school algebra classes

will never be needed in non-science

and non-technical professions.

Solving simple math mysteries

gives children practice looking at clues,

discovering patterns, and

struggling to gain insights.

That's what algebra is really about.

And that's what real-life problem solving is about.

Biggest Math Secret Number Two

Did you get caught in the trap of

thinking about division as if

it's mostly about splitting a whole pizza

into equal-size slices?

And dealing with fractions?

If so, your math teachers failed

to explain that division is a tool

that has many different uses.

As an analogy, the excessive focus

on division as a tool for splitting

is like giving someone a multi-tool

or swiss army knife and only showing

them how to use the knife blade.

The many other fold-out tools within

the multi-tool are also available,

but ignored, and unused.

So here's the next big secret.

When you get stumped by a real-life math problem,

solving the math probably involves division.

Why division? If the math just involves

addition and subtraction, the process for

solving the math problem would be obvious.

That's what basic accounting is,

and accounting is easy to understand.

Multiplication might be needed,

but that's also easy to recognize.

It's a shortcut for adding the

same number again and again.

But division is different.

To use the mystery metaphor,

division has many disguises and aliases.

Division is especially useful for comparing.

A simple example is calculating

miles per hour.

A car's distance traveled is

divided by the time it takes

to travel that distance.

If someone expresses their speed

as seventeen miles in twenty-three minutes,

that's difficult to compare with

another person who traveled those

seventeen miles in twenty-seven minutes.

It's much easier to use division

and then compare fourty four miles per hour

with thirty eight miles per hour.

The word "per" in miles per hour

reveals the need for division.

Similarly the word "percent"

provides another way to compare numbers.

Here are yet more words

that indicate a need for division:

proportion, scale, ratio, rate,

slope, and reciprocal.

Remember these terms.

They reveal that division is involved.

Alas, most schools fail to fully teach

the "why" behind division.

This failure is partly why so many students

struggle to understand chemistry,

physics, trigonometry, and statistics.

Another source of confusion comes from

using multiple different symbols for division.

Young students need an early introduction

to the slash symbol they will be using

on calculators, computer keyboards, and spreadsheets.

Here's a final tip about division.

If you don't know whether the bigger

or smaller number is entered first

or second on a calculator,

ask yourself if the result of division

should be bigger than one,

or smaller than one.

If bigger, the bigger number

is entered first.

If the result should be smaller than

one, the smaller number is entered first.

Real-life math problems would become

so much easier to solve if students

were taught to understand

what division really does.

It does a lot. It's especially

useful for doing comparisons.

Biggest Math Secret Number Three

When someone requests filling their

glass "three fourths full,"

that's clear. In contrast,

if that person said to fill it

"seventy five percent full,"

that would be confusing, or snobbish, or both.

This example reveals that fractions

are very useful for communication.

Fractions are especially useful

in recipies, such as telling you

to combine a half teaspoon of salt

with one and a half cups of flour.

However, calculating with fractions

should never be needed.

This brings us to the third

big math secret.

A fraction is a division task

that has not yet been done.

This means you should do the division

for each fraction before you

combine numeric fractions

using addition or subtraction.

Unfortunately schools waste

countless hours teaching students

to do the opposite.

They teach kids how to

add and subtract fractions.

That's not a useful skill in real life.

You might disagree if you are a carpenter

who uses a metal tape measure,

or if you work with clothes using

a cloth tape measure.

In these cases, please keep watching

because the next secret

will save you lots of time.

When you need to use a fraction

in a calculation, start by

dividing the two numbers in the fraction.

In contrast, if the fraction

is being used for communication,

there's no need to do division.

Biggest Math Secret Number Four

Possibly the biggest loss of joy

in math education happens when

young students learn to use a ruler

marked in quarters, eighths,

and possibly sixteenths of an inch.

Splitting an inch into quarters

and eighths has been obsolete for

at least a few centuries.

Yet students in the United States

are required to buy rulers

with these obsolete markings.

Unfortunately some professions still

expect you to use fractional inches.

Carpentry and sewing are two such professions.

If you still use rulers or tape

measures marked with fractions,

it's time to buy a ruler and

tape measure with marks for decimal inches.

You will be amazed at how much easier

it is to add and subtract decimal inches.

Some educators think we should avoid

fractional inches by switching to

the metric system. Nope!

That misunderstanding is explained

in the next math secret.

Instead, schools should be asking

students to buy a ruler that shows

decimal inches, where each

interval between marks is

one tenth of an inch.

The pattern of decimal inches is simple.

Find the larger tic mark

at half an inch. Then count

the smaller tic marks two at a time.

Those tic marks are named point two,

point four, point six, and point eight.

This same pattern in a smaller

size applies to centimeters.

This means learning decimal inches

makes it much easier

to learn the metric system.

Here's another important change.

School rulers should go up to

ten inches, not twelve inches.

When the topic of percentages arise,

the ten-inch ruler will have provided

a useful way to judge percentages

from zero to one hundred.

Students will immediately realize

each tenth of an inch is simply

one percent of the

ruler's total marked length.

As another advantage a decimal ruler

makes it easy to add and subtract

integers up to one hundred.

Addition goes to the right.

Subtraction goes to the left.

The bigger marks keep track of

the tens digit, the smaller tic marks

keep track of the units digit.

Workers in the construction industry

already can buy a "decimal tape measure,"

which is sometimes called

an "engineering" tape measure.

Alas, for people who do sewing,

cloth tape measures marked in decimal inches

are currently very hard to find.

That will change soon.

Switching between decimal inches

and fractional inches is easy

if you use a tape measure that shows both.

Just look across to the opposite

side when you need to convert.

Here's the trick for teaching

negative numbers. Print onto paper

a decimal ruler that goes from

negative five inches at the left

to positive five inches at the right,

with zero in the middle.

Then ask students to count up

and down on the decimal tic marks.

Sometimes they will temporarily

be pointing to the left of the

zero point, before returning back

into positive numbers.

This exposure to temporary

negative numbers will help prepare

them to later learn how to

add and subtract negative numbers.

Let's stop teaching students

to measure inches using fractions.

That skill became obsolete centuries ago

when decimal numbers arrived.

Biggest Math Secret Number Five

Europeans use the metric system,

which measures time in seconds.

Yet two European friends don't say

"Bye, see you in six hundred and

four point eight kiloseconds"

instead of saying "Bye, see you

in a week." Everyone, worldwide,

speaks using the same familiar

time units we use in the United States,

namely minutes, hours, days,

weeks, months, and years.

Some people think we should adopt

metric units in the kitchen

for recipies and food prep.

Yet that misses the point.

Recipes in the United States can

continue to specify quantities in

tablespoons, teaspoons, cups,

pints, quarts, and gallons.

Anyone who wants to switch to

different units in the kitchen

can take a picture or screenshot of

any recipie and ask artificial intelligence

to convert it, including all

the measurements in all the steps,

into any units they want.

Elsewhere we can continue to use

yards for football fields,

feet for cutting lumber,

and inches for clothing sizes.

Only when we need to do calculations

do we need to choose just one unit

for each measurement category.

This concept is similar to the

distinction between fractions and

decimal numbers. Fractions are fine

for communication, but decimals

are needed for calculations.

In a similar way, any unit can be

used for communication, but choosing

just one unit per category

is necessary for doing calculations.

Here's a final tip about units.

When you don't have a computerized way

to convert units, you can multiply

a number times the equivalent of

the number one. For example,

sixty minutes per hour is essentially

the number one. So you can

mulitiply sixty times a number

in miles per minute to

convert into miles per hour.

Let's teach students to communicate[!]

using whatever measuring unit

fits the situation. And let's teach

students that calculations[!]

require converting each supplied number

of the same type into the same unit.

Then, after calculating, that unit

can be be converted back into

any meaningful unit for communication.

Biggest Math Secret Number Six

Even before children learn how to add

and subtract they can draw three lines

on a sheet of paper. And they can

be taught to draw these lines along

a straightedge, which can be a

piece of wood or plastic that has

no numbers or markings.

And they can recognize the shape

of three intersecting lines as a triangle.

At that point they are ready

to learn to draw a map.

Indoors the map can be of the

classroom or an assembly hall.

Outdoors the map can be of

fenceposts and corners of buildings.

To draw this map there must be

two tables that are not too close

to each other. On a piece of paper

the child draws two Xs where they

imagine the tables will be located

on their completed map. Then,

using the straightedge, they need

to draw a line between those two Xs.

At each table the child needs to

rotate the paper so the first line

points to the other table.

Then the child draws lines that

point to distant places such as

room corners or fenceposts.

These lines must pass through

the X that represents the

table they are currently at.

That same process is repeated

at the other table.

The place where two lines to the

same location cross is the

location of that wall's corner

or fencepost. The walls and

fences can be drawn as straight

lines between those points.

After these children have later learned

how to divide two numbers, they can

use their decimal rulers to measure

distances on their maps.

Then they can use division and

multiplication to measure

actual distances.

The magic of measuring distances

without being able to walk along

the measured distances will

bring joy and valuable math insights.

This map-making can be combined

with their algebraic mystery-solving skills.

They will realize they can measure

the height of a tall tree or

building without climbing.

That just uses a triangle

that's oriented vertically.

Here the math secret is that

a deep understanding of triangles

at an early age will make it

much easier to understand

troublesome math topics including

division, trigonometry, graphing,

geometry, physics, and even calculus.

In fact, the trigonometric functions

named sine, cosine, and tangent

are just ratios among the sides

of a right triangle.

Arcsine, arccosine, and arctangent

simply convert ratios back

into corresponding angles.

When students learn about graphing

X and Y points, they will realize

the edges of the paper have been

playing the role of the X and Y axes.

This means a specific point on

the paper has an X value equal to

the the distance to the paper's left edge,

and has a Y value equal to the

distance to the paper's bottom edge.

When these students reach high-school

geometry courses, they can more quickly

learn college-entrance geometry topics

such as the cleverest steps in

Euclid's proofs, and non-Euclidean geometry.

When they reach a physics course

they will already be familiar with the

concept of a slope, which simply equals

the ratio between horizontal

distance and vertical distance.

When they reach a calculus course

they might recognize that a derivative,

which is expressed as dy divided by dx,

can be thought of as the height

of a right triangle divided by

the width of that triangle.

Let's start teaching foundational

concepts about triangles much earlier.

Biggest Math Secret Number Seven

Which math event led to the decline of

highway robbery and the begining

of the Italian Renaissance?

Highway robbery was a normal part

of travel back when Romans

built the first highways.

Back then, merchants and wealthy Romans

had to carry coins, jewels,

and other valuable objects

when they traveled between cities.

That's because ancient Romans

did math using Roman numerals,

and popes opposed adopting the new

Hindu-Arabic numerals.

Later, adopting the digits zero through nine

became the math event that made it possible

to create banks that served multiple cities.

Increasingly travelers between cities

carried fewer coins and jewels and

more often carried bank documents.

That's when highway robbery

began to decline significantly.

Safer travel between cities

increased economic prosperity.

And it increased the flow of ideas,

including artistic influence.

These developments helped lead to

the Italian Renaissance.

This math history helps children

appreciate that decimal numbers

are worth learning because they are

the foundation of improved communication

and much better living conditions.

Another useful history perspecitve

is that algebra's

"order of operations" notation

developed back when mathematicians

wrote equations using primitive

technologies such as a quill pen

and, before that,

a table sprinkled with sawdust or sand.

Now you understand why learning

the order of operations

is not needed for "doing math."

Use as many parentheses as you want.

Or draw rectangles to show grouping.

Since this video has thirteen

biggest math secrets, it's worth

mentioning why hotels don't have

a thirteenth floor.

Dividing the number thirteen by

any other number was frustrating.

back before decimal numbers arrived.

The centuries-old reputation of

thirteen as an unlucky number

would make the thirteenth floor

of a hotel a tempting target for

mischevious young troublemakers.

Which helps perpetuate the belief

that thirteen is a number worth avoiding.

Math history, if taught well,

is not only facinating and insightful,

it's very inspiring.

So let's use math-history stories

to inspire joy and appreciation

for what math teachers can teach us.

Biggest Math Secret Number Eight

Calculus is breathtakingly beautiful.

Using it led to being able to calculate

the exact position of a planet

at any moment in time.

Integrals allow us to exactly calculate

the area under a polynomial curve.

Derivatives allow us to exactly calculate

the speed of a falling object

at every moment during its fall.

Yet for many people climbing the

"advanced-placement" mountain

to glimpse the beauty of calculus

consumes too much time, effort,

and money considering that

very very few professions

require using calculus for any calculations.

In a similar way, advanced algebra

is filled with beautiful concepts

that involve vectors, matrices,

advanced trigonometry, and imaginary numbers.

Yet these topics also aren't used

in hardly any professions.

So why have schools built a

well-traveled road to the top

of a mountain for a quick view

of the mathematical beauty

in calculus and advanced algebra?

There are two reasons.

The more noble reason is the hope

of training future scientists

who will pioneer advances in

software, electronics, chemistry,

biochemistry, aviation, satellites,

and space travel. And they want

to train future engineers

who will pioneer new products,

better bridges, faster computers,

and better aircraft.

These frontiers have become

the battlegrounds for nations

competing against each other

for economic advantages,

and for backup military advantages.

This is why lots of tax money

is spent on education

for training scientists and engineers.

The uglier reason for focusing on

seldom-used math concepts

is that lots of businesses

earn lots of money testing students

on the advanced math topics

of calculus, advanced algebra,

and advanced trigonometry.

Additional businesses profit from

preparing students to take

college-entrance exams.

And universities can gain prestige

and charge higher tuition

if more of their graduates

enter high-paying professions

that involve higher levels of math.

Fortunately the most useful parts

of basic calculus and some

advanced algebra can be

moved into earlier grade levels.

This approach will free up time

for students to learn real-life skills

in high school that are currently

untaught, yet useful.

Especially useful would be to teach

non-math problem solving skills.

This skill has suddenly become more

important now that artificial intelligence

can easily solve most math problems.

A few people who financially benefit

from existing math-education traditions

will fear loosing some financial income.

Yet everyone on our planet will benefit

from having more scientists and

mathematicians working to help

businesses reach higher levels

of economic prosperity,

and help create solutions to

the many problems civilization now faces.

Biggest Math Secret Number Nine

When scientists and engineers design

an innovative structure they don't think

of the name "Pythagorean theorem"

every time they use the concept that,

for a right triangle, the sum of the

squares of the two shorter sides

equals the square of the longer side.

Yet math tests assume a student who

doesn't remember this name does not

understand the concept.

Let's keep things in perspective.

Multiple civilizations discovered

this concept and used it for many

centuries before Pythagorus was born.

Why do math textbooks and

standardized tests put so much

emphasis on terminology?

Up until a few decades ago,

before videos and audio recordings

became inexpensive to produce,

watch, and listen to,

knowledge was written in books.

An easy way to find out if a

student had read a chapter

in a textbook was to ask questions

about the definitions of terms

defined in the chapter.

For this reason, textbook authors,

who are experts in their academic fields,

are told to include terminology

that they, the experts, do not

themselves use regularly

in their own academic field.

Here the big secret is that

lots and lots of math terminology

is not useful to lots of people who work

in the worlds of business, travel,

entertainment, and government.

And excess terminology is not useful

in a kitchen or workshop.

Instead it's the concepts behind the words

that are important to learn.

For example, after college

most people don't need to remember

the precise definitions for the words

proportion, scale, ratio,

rate, slope, and reciprocal.

It's the underlying concepts

those words convey

that are extremely valuable.

When advanced terminology is important,

definitions are readily available

by searching Wikipedia

and asking for assistance from

artificial intelligence services.

This means math education

should reduce the number of math terms

being taught in school.

When video and audio learning resources

are created, they should

avoid making the same mistake.

Let's remember that insightful

math concepts are much more important

than seldom-used terminology

and precise definitions.

Biggest Math Secret Number Ten

Multiple choice tests are obsolete.

This is the next math secret.

Rather than wasting time looking at

the many limitations of

multiple-choice tests,

let's consider this question.

"What will replace multiple-choice tests?"

Those tests will be replaced by

computer software that uses elements

of artificial intelligence to provide

each and every math student with

a private math tutor.

That software will be free,

tiny, and capable of both teaching

and testing at the same time.

This is the kind of tutoring

that Alexander the Great

received from Aristotle.

It's the kind of tutoring

that kids from wealthy families

get from privately hired math tutors.

Math teachers have a favorite mantra

to "show your work."

Well-designed software will accomplish

this goal by following a student

through each step the student takes.

For this reason the software will

include an on-screen calculator

so the software can pay attention

to how it's used.

Whenever the student struggles

to understand a new concept,

the software will not just recognize

the lack of understanding.

It will immediately give the student

yet another way to understand the concept.

This development will force universities

to change how they choose who to admit.

Probably universities will switch to

similar software to test the

mental skills of potential students.

This improved testing will shift away

from asking students to recall

carefully worded definitions

of advanced terminology.

Instead the focus will shift to

assessing how deeply the student

understands fundamental math concepts.

And how quickly the student

can learn new concepts.

The math-testing industry will

fight against this development.

Yet ultimately the fight will be won

by cultures and nations

that prioritize teaching over testing.

Those are the cultures and nations

that will more efficiently invent

economically valuable technology

and useful innovations.

After all, that is the most important

reason for spending money on public education.

Biggest Math Secret Number Eleven

You've probably seen the math

equation E equals M C squared.

Notice the spoken version of this

equation uses the word "squared."

In contrast, the written version

writes the digit two as a superscript

written in a smaller font.

In prior centuries, writing on paper

and printing on paper was the only

affordable and convenient way to

communicate math equations.

As a result, the notation for

math equations developed in ways

that are awkward to speak,

yet convenient to write and print.

Now that we can speak into microphones

it's easier to say "energy equals

mass times speed of light squared."

But that takes longer to write.

Math notation is slowly developing

to allow math information to

be typed and spoken.

During this transition let's not

insist students must only use

the notation conventions

used by mathematicians and scientists.

The secret here is that math teachers

need to admit that written math notation

is becoming obsolete for non-math professions.

Without this secret being revealed,

students assume they are to blame

for struggling to learn math notation.

Scientists, mathematicians, and engineers

can continue to use traditional

written math notation.

That doesn't need to change.

However, students going into other

professions should be allowed

to learn math concepts without

also learning a notation system

that is failing to keep up with

recent technical developments.

Computer programming shares

a similar challenge.

Most computer programming languages

cannot be spoken into a microphone

because they use lots of symbols

and punctuation marks.

An important challenge shared by

math and programming languages

is being able to indicate grouping.

For example, division uses a

long horizontal line to show

that one group of values are divided

by another group of values.

What is the spoken version

of this grouping?

Computer programming languages use

parentheses for these groupings.

This allows the words

"open paren" and "close paren"

to be used to indicate groupings.

The Dashrep programming lanugage,

which is speakable, uses the words

"ambee" and "amenn" to indicate

the beginning and ending

of a nested group.

We don't know what words eventually

will be used to indicate grouping verbally.

We do know that lots of what schools teach

must somehow make the transition

from written language to spoken language.

Especially now that

artificial intelligence has been

trained to understand spoken language.

Biggest Math Secret Number Twelve

Suppose you don't know how to swim.

Would you choose to wade across a river

that has an average depth

of sixteen inches?

Would your choice change if

the median depth is also

sixteen inches?

Would also knowing the

standard deviation help you decide?

Statistics courses spend lots of time

teaching how to characterize lots

of data by reducing it to

just a few numbers.

Sometimes that's very useful.

Often it's misleading.

To answer the question about

wading across a river,

what you really need is a map.

Ideally it would be a topographic

map that shows river depth at all locations.

Then you will know

where it's safe to wade across.

Notice the numbers for average, median,

and standard deviation

aren't useful for this purpose.

Averages, medians, and standard deviations

serve to characterize what's called

a normal distribution.

Yet real life seldom involves

normal distributions.

In fact, when you're trying to solve

a real-life problem, probably

a normal distribution doesn't apply.

That's why unsolved problems

typically are challenging.

Being able to calculate a number

is just a beginning.

We constantly need to recognize

what the number really means.

For example, if you have a financial

investment that increases by fifteen percent,

and then decreases by fifteen percent,

have you gained money,

lost money, or are you back

to your original dollar amount?

Even after studying statistics,

most people have to think carefully

about this question.

This deeper kind of thinking

is what's needed. Unfortunately

lots of people want to quickly assume

the percent numbers can be added

and subtracted, as if an increase

of fifteeen percent followed by

a decrease of fifteen percent

yields no change.

Instead, deeper thinking reveals

the result is a smaller number.

Numbers are great. They give us information.

Yet numbers, especially statistics,

also make it easy to believe we know

more than what we actually know.

So we always need to dig deep

to reach a clear understanding.

Biggest Math Secret Number Thirteen

Geometry is filled with beautiful images

and beautiful concepts.

Yet most geometry courses torture students

with lots of words, and few images. Why?

Centuries ago a Greek named Euclid

wrote a series of mathematical proofs

that beautifully demonstrate rigorous thinking

about parallel lines, angles, and triangles.

Schools and teachers want to inspire

this rigorous thinking in students.

That's a noble goal.

Unfortunately math teachers

and geometry textbooks excessively focus

on aspects of Euclid's proofs that can be

tested using multiple-choice tests.

This is the big secret of geometry.

The beauty and joyous potential of geometry

has been hijacked

by the testing industry.

And by word-oriented textbooks,

which are easier to create

than graphic-focused books.

Fortunately we now have access to computers

and free software for drawing

lines and shapes that previously

were drawn with awkward drawing tools.

Better yet, free software is now available

to create animated graphic videos

that demonstrate important geometry concepts.

This means advanced students can

create animated geometry videos,

including ones that show

highlights of Euclid's proofs.

Here is an animation that shows

how to remember how to calculate

a triangle's area.

The area is half the area of

the surrounding rectangle.

Graphics, including animated graphic videos,

can help students learn rigorous thinking

skills within the field of geometry.

Then, after graduation, that rigorous thinking

can help them solve problems in the real world.

What do these thirteen secrets

have in common? They reveal

that math education follows

very primitive, outdated,

and inefficient ways of teaching math.

Students know that math education

is fundamentally flawed.

One of their clues that something is wrong

is that lots of parents can't help

their children with math homework.

So they ask "If it's so important

to learn, why didn't you learn it?"

It's time to start teaching what students

really need for solving real-life

math problems as an adult.

If you're a teacher you can begin

to teach your favorite of these math secrets.

If you're an adult, notice you are now

less afraid of math. So take your time

the next time you encounter

If you're a student you can focus

your attention on the important

fundametals and underlying concepts of math.
